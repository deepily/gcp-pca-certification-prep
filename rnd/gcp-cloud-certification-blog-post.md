# **GCP Cloud Architect Certification Prep: Towards a More Sustainable Approach**

## **Who Doesn't Love Studying Evenings and Weekends?**

I'll bet you $100 that very few of us actually love studying for their obligatory professional development certification exams in our off-the-clock hours. I certainly don't, and as a former educator, I recognize the importance of dialing down the resentment level before attempting to learn anything as *"negative affect most assuredly engenders negative learning outcomes"*.

In my case, the end of 2024 saw me dashing madly about trying to crush an entire PMLE's (Google Cloud Professional Machine Learning Engineer) worth of product trivia and learning into my head in two months at the end of my first year at Google.

I swore I would never repeat that experience again, so here I am, telling you how I did my best to reduce all the cognitive toil associated with checking off yet another end of year requirement.

## **Breathless Podcasts vs. A Big Faceful of Book**

The first time I went through the certification process, I remember asking *“how am I going to get all that dry PMLE content into my head if I’m already brain damaged by the end of my 50 to 60 hour work weeks?”* My best cognitive capacity had already been consumed, so I needed an easier, low friction way of ingesting the information.

Fortunately for me, [NotebookLM](https://notebooklm.google.com/) had recently been introduced, so I scraped a chapter's worth of material from the [PMLE](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Official+Google+Cloud+Certified+Professional+Machine+Learning+Engineer+Study+Guide-p-9781119981565) e-book and threw it into the bin so that it could produce a breathless dynamic duo podcast based on the first chapter of the prep book. I found it amusing to listen to those two gush over the dry chapter’s material, along with the chapter's questions and answers while I was commuting by bike or Metro, working out at the gym, or even going for walks.

The audio banter, I mean *information*, entered my head like a glass of cold water goes down on a hot day: easily, and refreshing. This is where I developed a second bit of intuition: as I made fun of the relentlessly relatable interplay between NotebookLM’s dynamic duo podcasts, I began to predict what their next words would be. That little trick is worth its weight in gold, because now I was basically tricking myself into paying attention and really engaging with what they were saying to the point where on the second playback of the Q\&A podcast, I was answering before the correct answer was provided. Ding, ding, ding\! I had found a winning strategy.

## **My System: An Iterative Learning Grind**

Now that I had found an easier way to get the MLPE book into my head, I began hand-curating data sources using multiple copy and pastes of each chapter's content, questions and answers and pitching them into plain text files. While the process is simple and easily described – *it is* *tedious* – it took me more than a few hours to hand curate and wait for the audio podcasts to be generated. Despite this self-inflicted toil, I decided that in [sharing my podcasts with others](https://drive.google.com/drive/folders/1BHtN_iXE7yr_8tzqASXBUV34Mk79X0H_?usp=sharing) I could amortize the upfront costs.

After setting up my little cookie cutter podcast creation process – and wishing that I could script it instead of using my little digital hammer and chisel – I needed a way of measuring how much I was actually learning, so I took the online assessment exam to get a baseline: 60% without any preparation. Not bad\!

**The chapter grind**: The outer loop consists of iterating through all chapters, processing each individual chapter thusly: 

* Scrape, prompt and build chapter \+ Q\&A podcasts  
* Listen to the chapter podcast once   
* Review the Q\&A podcast twice, using active listening  
* Take the chapter exam  
* Review incorrect chapter exam answer-based podcasts twice, using active listening

After processing all 12 chapters at the rate of two chapters per week, over the next two weeks I worked on the two practice exams and progressed from an initial score of 80% to a final one of 95%. 

**My two practice exam two-step**:

* Take a practice exam  
* Take screenshots of the questions that I got wrong and build yet another podcast   
* Review the incorrect answers, listening to the podcast twice, actively  
* Re-take the exam and confirm score improvement 

## **The Relative Perception of Time: Hastily vs. Leisurely**

I’ve been through this process twice in the last two years, the first time I did it in about two months. The second time around I relaxed, took my time and prepped about a chapter a week \+ practice exams over a period of four months, and the difference was astounding: it was actually easy to do all the prep this time around. This is worth repeating: *it was easy\!*

## **A Handy Little Hack: Multiple-Choice Mastery**

The standard advice is to discard two of the four options by looking for obviously bad answers, leaving you with 50-50 odds. But you're not flipping a coin – you're either choosing the best answer or eliminating the worst of the two remaining, so you've got reasoning and learning to increase your odds of getting it right.

What hasn't been emphasized is the need to practice your elimination skills from the very beginning. For my second exam prep I practiced this from my very first chapter exam, and it turned out to be a lifesaver: Quite unintentionally, I managed to get only five hours of sleep the night before my certification exam and was miserably tired during the test, struggling to pay attention and stay awake to the very end.

Luckily, I had already laid down a long series of cognitive reps through multiple exposures to the material over time with high engagement and low duress. My learning equation representing this method is: ***L \= F × I × T × (1/Duress)*** 

***L**earning \= **F**requency of exposure \* **I**ntensity of exposure \* **T**ime \* (inverse of the amount of **D**uress you experience while studying)*

I had not only maximized this learning equation but also built automaticity by repeatedly handling scenario-based multiple-choice questions. It was this automaticity that carried me through my exhausted Friday afternoon and allowed me to pass this exam under decidedly sub-optimal cognitive circumstances.

## **Distributions of Prep Materials vs. Exam Content**

It's worth commenting on what I saw in the distribution of my preparatory dataset compared to the exam itself: While the certification podcasts spent quite a bit of time discussing choosing the correct database, there was much less of this on the actual test. Additionally, there were more mentions of Kubernetes/GKE clusters along with Apigee mentions than I remember hearing in the preparatory material.

If you want to seek out additional GKE clustering scenarios, it wouldn't hurt. It also wouldn't hurt to spend some time understanding what Apigee is – which never came up in the podcasts –  along with familiarizing yourself with the case studies mentioned in the training material.

## **Takeaways: Actionable & Obvious Wisdom**

My two words of advice for you? Start now.

Additional advice: practice scenario-based question answering, eliminating the two obviously bad choices first.

As far as my methodology of iteratively listening and interacting with audio podcasts? Do as you see fit, as we all have different learning styles and by now you've evolved a series of best practices that work for you.

I'm betting, however, that as an adjunct to your particular approach, my podcasts should provide an easy supplement to your studies as you go about your daily – and somewhat mindless – tasks like commuting, doing the dishes, laundry, or even cleaning the house. There are plenty of already physically automatic things that we do on a daily basis that would permit you to actively engage with audio content whilst still getting other things done.

My final words of advice? Get a good night's sleep\!
